Short-term rental villas in Bali commonly generate gross yields of 8–15%, but net yields after management, maintenance, taxes, and realistic occupancy typically land between 5% and 10%. That is still strong compared to most Western markets, but the gap between the gross number a seller quotes and the net return you actually keep is where most investors get surprised.

Here is how to model Bali rental income honestly, so your spreadsheet matches reality.

## What is a realistic rental yield in Bali?

A realistic net yield for a well-located, well-managed Bali villa is 5–10% per year. Gross figures of 12–15% are achievable in prime short-term rental areas, but they assume high nightly rates and strong occupancy before any costs. Once you subtract management fees, upkeep, taxes, and the occupancy you will actually achieve, the net number is meaningfully lower. Underwrite to the net figure, never the gross.

## Why is the net yield lower than the advertised gross?

Because the advertised gross ignores the real cost of running a villa. The main drains on gross revenue are:

| Cost | Typical impact on revenue |
|---|---|
| Management fees | 15–25% |
| Occupancy gap (vs 90% quoted) | 20–30% |
| Maintenance, pool, garden | 5–10% |
| Utilities and supplies | 5–8% |
| Taxes (rental + PBB) | varies |
| Furnishing refresh (amortized) | 3–5% |

Stack these up and a "12% gross" villa often nets 6–8%. The investors who do well are the ones who budgeted for all of this before buying.

## How should I model occupancy?

Model 60–70% occupancy for a typical short-term rental villa, not the 85–90% sellers like to quote. Occupancy depends on location, seasonality, the quality of your listing and photos, your management team, and competition in your micro-area. New villas also take time to build reviews and ranking on booking platforms, so first-year occupancy is usually lower. Build a conservative base case and a stretch case, and make sure the deal works in the base case.

## A simple worked example

Consider a USD 300,000 villa in an emerging Bali area:

1. **Average nightly rate**: USD 180
2. **Occupancy**: 65% → ~237 nights/year
3. **Gross revenue**: ~USD 42,700 (≈14% gross)
4. **Management (20%)**: −USD 8,540
5. **Maintenance, utilities, supplies (15%)**: −USD 6,400
6. **Taxes and fees (est.)**: −USD 3,500
7. **Net income**: ~USD 24,260 → **~8% net yield**

An 8% net yield on a hard asset in a growing market is excellent — but notice it came from a 14% gross figure. That compression is normal and expected.

## What drives higher yields?

The biggest levers, in order of impact:

1. **Location** — proximity to beaches, dining, and demand hubs.
2. **Management quality** — the difference between 55% and 70% occupancy.
3. **Listing quality** — professional photos and pricing strategy.
4. **Design and amenities** — a strong pool and aesthetic command premium rates.
5. **Buying well** — a lower purchase price lifts every yield figure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are Bali rental yields better than long-term rentals?

Short-term (nightly) rentals usually produce higher gross yields than long-term leases but require active management and carry occupancy risk. Long-term rentals to expats produce lower but steadier income with far less operational effort. The right choice depends on how hands-on you want to be.

### Do I pay tax on rental income in Indonesia?

Yes. Rental income is taxable, and the applicable rate and mechanism depend on your residency status and whether you operate through a PT PMA. Annual land and building tax (PBB) also applies. Work with an Indonesian tax advisor to structure this correctly.

### How long until a Bali villa pays back?

At a 7–9% net yield, the income alone returns your capital over roughly 11–14 years, before any appreciation. Capital growth in strong areas can shorten the effective payback considerably, but should be treated as upside, not the base case.
